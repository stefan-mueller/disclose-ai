Information for Lecturers

This page answers the questions lecturers have asked most often after testing the AI Disclosure Generator with their own students.

Where do these categories come from?

The categories in Step 1 of the tool are drawn from task categories used in student surveys and existing AI disclosure guidelines. They form a checklist of common use cases, not a recommendation that students use AI for all, or indeed any, of them. Whether AI is appropriate for a given category still depends entirely on your assignment brief and your own instructions.

Step 1 is the crucial filtering step for students: it should reflect only what your assignment or course/module actually permits, not everything AI happens to be capable of. Students who are unsure whether a category applies are told to leave it unticked and ask you rather than guess.

How can I phrase permitted AI use in my assignment brief?

Some lecturers find it useful to name the same categories in their assignment brief as the ones in Step 1, rather than describing permitted and prohibited AI use in separate wording. Students can then tick exactly what has been permitted without having to interpret how a general policy applies to their situation.

One way this can look in an assignment brief: “For this assignment, you may use AI for Conceptualisation and Initial literature screening, for instance to brainstorm ideas or locate relevant sources. AI must not be used for Data management, Coding and automation, Writing and editing, or Audiovisual and interactive components, as these should be your own unassisted work. Anything else can be recorded under Additional tasks. Please use the AI Disclosure Generator to record your AI use and attach the generated declaration to your assignment submission: https://disclose-ai.app.”

What if my course/module has not settled on an AI policy yet?

A student who ticks “no guidance” in Step 1 is told to treat that as AI not being permitted, rather than as permission by default, until you or the course/module coordinator confirms otherwise. Settling on even a brief policy, using the wording above, removes that ambiguity for your students.

How do I cite this tool?

If you are using this tool in your teaching, you can cite it as:

Müller, Stefan. AI Disclosure Generator. https://disclose-ai.app

Related literature and advice

A number of similar tools exist to support transparent AI-use disclosure, including the AID Framework, the GAIDeT Declaration Generator, and sIfA (Statement of Intellectual Fellowship and Accountability), a browser-based tool from Busara that maps AI contributions onto the 14 CRediT contributor roles and is aimed more at researchers documenting a publication than at student coursework. All aim at the same goal: helping students and researchers disclose their AI use clearly and consistently. Use whichever fits your context best.

The optional “AIAS level” field in the tool uses the AI Assessment Scale, a complementary framework instructors use to specify how much AI use is permitted on a given assessment; this tool only lets you record that level, it doesn’t set or enforce it.

This tool’s approach is comparable to the American Journal of Political Science’s AI Policy, which asks authors to describe how AI was used rather than reproduce full transcripts. If you’re an instructor who wants students to submit the entire output of their AI conversations, not just a summary and the prompts used, ask for that separately.
